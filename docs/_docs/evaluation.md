# Evaluation

What VIK costs, measured two ways, against the solvers a verification tool would otherwise call. The first
way is the one that matters when the solver is embedded: the process is already running and warm, and each
question is a call. The second is the one competitions measure: a new process per question, start-up
included.

Everything here is reproducible from the repository; the commands are at the end, and the raw data is
committed next to the results.

## What was measured

Sixty-one instances. Forty-four are of the [SMT-LIB library](https://smt-lib.org), release 2025, but for one
generated here and two dumped by the Scala 3 compiler's qualified-types checker — spread over the logics VIK
supports and small enough that the comparison is about the solvers rather than about one hard instance: eight
`QF_UF`, eight `QF_LIA`, six `QF_UFLIA`, six with datatypes (three of them `QF_UFDTLIA`), six `QF_BV`, five over
the reals, five with arrays. Two are `QF_AUFBVDT` files from elsewhere (`corpus/QF_AUFBVDT/other`): the bit
stream of the verified ASN.1/ACN serialization work, and the reproduction of
[Stainless issue 1801](https://github.com/epfl-lara/stainless/issues/1801). Fifteen are solver sessions of
[Stainless](https://github.com/epfl-lara/stainless) (`corpus/<logic>/stainless`): what its unrolling issues to
one incremental solver while it verifies one function — from three assertions and one `check-sat` to two
thousand assertions and forty-seven `check-sat`s, most of them over datatypes, functions and integers together
(`QF_UFDTLIA`, nearly two thirds of its 3 580 sessions and five of the fifteen), the others with bit-vectors or
arrays too. The fifteen were chosen to spread over the time VIK takes on them, from a millisecond to past the
budget, to hold the sessions the solver's recent changes move the most, faster and slower, and to hold sessions
where z3 is far ahead of VIK. They are picked, not sampled: the changes they were chosen for move them more
than they move Stainless's sessions as a whole, and a claim about Stainless is to be measured on all 3 580
([`StainlessReplay`](https://github.com/mbovel/vik/blob/main/bench/src/main/scala/io/bovel/bench/stainless/StainlessReplay.scala)).
Twenty-six of the sixty-one are incremental scripts — several `check-sat`s under
`push`/`pop` or `check-sat-assuming`, the
*Incremental* column — and there the whole script is the question. The two tables hold the same sixty-one, in
the same order
([`bench/evaluation-instances.txt`](https://github.com/mbovel/vik/blob/main/bench/evaluation-instances.txt)).
They are part of the
[corpus](https://github.com/mbovel/vik/tree/main/corpus) the tests play, with their families and licences.
The set is deliberately of the kind a verifier produces — many small queries — and it is not a sample of the
competition's hard tail, where VIK does not compete (see [limitations](#limitations)).

Two families of solvers appear:

- **VIK** on its three platforms: the JVM, a native binary (Scala Native, LLVM), and Node.js (Scala.js,
  linked to WebAssembly); and, when `bench/graal.sh` has built one, as a GraalVM native image.
- **Others**: z3 and cvc5 (the binaries, and in process through the JavaSMT bindings — from text and
  through their APIs), princess and SMTInterpol (JVM solvers, in process and from the command line).

## Warm: the solver as a library

The measurement is JMH in the same JVM: three forks, three warm-up and three measurement iterations of one
second each, average time per operation, one thread. Every operation is a complete answer — parse the script,
build the problem, solve it — on a solver built for that call, which is what a verification tool does per
query.

**Times in milliseconds — lower is better;** the best cell of a row is green. Every measurement here holds
to a ten-second budget: a solver past it on an instance is halted, noted on
[`bench/timeouts.txt`](https://github.com/mbovel/vik/blob/main/bench/timeouts.txt), never run again, and its
cell reads 🐢 — past ten seconds per operation, the milliseconds of the others are the story. A `—` is a
cell deliberately left out, for the reasons below.

The 95% confidence intervals (Student's t over the samples of a cell) average 4% of the mean; every cell is within 20% of its mean except Princess (API) on `valid-BooleanOps__9` (24%), Princess (API) on `swap__swap_invalid_t3_np_nf_ai_00002_009.cvc` (21%), Z3 (API) on `20210219-Sydr__symbolic_memory__nested__cjpeg__predicate_150` (23%).

Every column receives the problem already built: *VIK (API)*, *Z3 (API)*, *CVC5 (API)*, *Princess (API)*
and *SMTInterpol (API)* are handed the script's own terms through each solver's API — declarations, terms,
asserts, `push`, `pop`, every `check-sat` and every `get-model` — which is what an embedding verifier pays. The same run also
measures VIK, z3 and cvc5 handed the *text* of the script, parsing it themselves (the `text` suites, in the
raw data linked below, and in the table on request: the `--columns` option of `bench/warm2md.py`); those
columns are not shown, the API being the faster side on nearly every instance, and the gap being the
parser. Each of the five
replays is checked against the solver's own front end run in process on the same text
([`ReplayCheck.scala`](https://github.com/mbovel/vik/blob/main/bench/src/main/scala/io/bovel/bench/evaluation/ReplayCheck.scala)):
the same answers, and a time within a factor of two — so what the solver receives is what its own parser
would have built, down to the order in which princess's parser flattens a nested `or` or `+` (handed the
same operands in another order, it took 27 s on `MESI_i4` where its parser takes 0.2 s; see
[external-issues.md](https://github.com/mbovel/vik/blob/main/external-issues.md)). The check also puts
parsing in proportion: over the forty instances the front end and the replay take about the same time —
the median ratio of the front end's time to the replay's is 1.01 for cvc5, 1.03 for princess, 1.19 for
SMTInterpol and 1.30 for z3, half of the instances within 0.8–1.5× of even — and z3's two suites say the
same, within 20% of each other either way on the small instances (the check's z3 front end builds a
context per run, 0.46 ms that dominates its ratio on the smallest). Parsing a script this size is
a small part of what a solver does with it, and z3's C++ parser builds a term about as fast as a JNI call
does. On the incremental instances z3's text suite
flattens the script into one problem — its bindings execute no script — a different question on a script
of many `check-sat`s, and its cells are left out. Princess is measured through its API for the same
reason in the other direction: its own script executor rejects these scripts (a re-declaration after
`pop`, see [external-issues.md](https://github.com/mbovel/vik/blob/main/external-issues.md)).

The 🐢 cells are mostly princess's — `NEQ004_size4`, `RandomDecoupled`, `lustre/ex3`, `car_6` and
`stack_k20` ask more than ten seconds per operation of it. The others are z3 on
`pspace/ndist.a.20000` — 20 000-bit vectors, which z3 bit-blasts whether it parses them or is handed them —
and VIK on the `Certora` instance, discussed below. In the cold table, princess stops on `CLEARSY__0020` at
the re-declaration above, so that cell is a `—`.

*VIK (API)* is the solver handed the script's terms through its `Session`, which is how a tool that
builds the terms itself calls it — the lowering to the solver's own language is part of the call, as it is
for any client. Its `text` suite, VIK parsing the script as well, adds tens of microseconds on the small
instances.

On the queries a verifier produces — the small ones, a millisecond or less — VIK is the fastest of the
five, by one to two orders of magnitude over z3 and cvc5: 6 µs where z3 takes 380 µs and cvc5 1.8 ms on
`bug-1_ab_reg_max`; SMTInterpol, the other solver with no native boundary to cross, is the closest at
18 µs, and ahead on the two `QF_AX` instances. That
is not a statement about the solving algorithms but about what a call costs: z3 keeps one context here, as
an embedding tool would, and still pays a fresh solver and a JNI crossing per term and per query (a context
of its own per query, what the previous runs measured, added 0.3 ms); cvc5 spends 0.4 ms initialising its
engine at the first assertion of every fresh solver; and both engines are built for much larger problems
than these.

Where the instance has real work in it, the ranking turns around, and by more than the factor of five the
project aims at: `dillig/20-4` is 9.3 ms for VIK against 3.3 ms for z3 and 0.8 ms for SMTInterpol,
`wisas/xs_8_8` 30 ms against 2.9 ms and 2.5 ms, `NEQ004_size4` 119 ms against 5.7 ms (SMTInterpol takes
124 ms there). The two `wisas` instances are the honest worst case here — an arrangement
of shared integer terms that grows with the square of their number — and they are in the table for that
reason; they are also where the arithmetic's [propagation of the bounds it already
knows](https://github.com/mbovel/vik/blob/main/bench/results/smt/2026-08-31-laraserver4-bound-propagation.md)
earns its keep (without it they measured half again slower).

The middle ground is where the design shows: `CAV_2009/20-vars` is 2.3 ms against 3.3 ms for z3 (and
0.3 ms for SMTInterpol), `convert-tiff2jpg` is five times and `prime_cone_unsat_3` thirty-five times faster
than z3, and the datatype instances are two orders of magnitude faster than princess and level with
SMTInterpol (which answers `blocksworld` three times faster) — the two other solvers written for the JVM.

The incremental scripts split. On `CLEARSY__0020` — 181 easy `check-sat`s — the z3 replay and VIK lead,
6 ms and 9 ms for the whole script against 13 ms for SMTInterpol, 65 ms for cvc5 and 1.2 s for princess:
each check is one more small query, and the solver now lives across all of them. `two_counters` (the first
twenty-five checks of a Lustre BMC unrolling), `cpachecker/diskperf` and the two checks of the bit-vector
script are the same story, VIK ahead at 5.3 ms, 0.4 ms and 0.7 ms. On `kind/MESI_i4` the five checks are
real work, and holding the state of the earlier ones costs more than it saves: 710 ms where z3's replay
needs 12 ms and SMTInterpol 15 ms (princess 1.0 s); the split repeats on `lustre/ex3` (511 ms against
239 ms and 111 ms), on `cpachecker/list_false` (239 ms against 10 ms and 13 ms) and, widest, on `car_6`
(2.5 s against 83 ms, 120 ms, and 1.1 s for cvc5). Datatypes and arrays under `push`/`pop` land in between:
`stack_k20` — forty checks of a bounded model check over a stack, generated here — is 82 ms against 7 ms
for z3 and 198 ms for SMTInterpol, ahead of cvc5's 409 ms, and the two `safari` array scripts are 168 ms
and 247 ms where z3 replays them in 13 ms and 28 ms, SMTInterpol in 14 ms and 45 ms, and cvc5 parses and
answers them in 48 ms and 137 ms. That price
is [measured and accepted](https://github.com/mbovel/vik/blob/main/bench/results/smt/2026-08-31-laraserver4-incremental.md):
one solver lives across the checks, its learned clauses with it, which is a win where the unsat steps of a
BMC unrolling repeat and a cost on `MESI_i4`, where the atoms of the popped levels stay registered and each
check pays for the history before it.

## Cold: a process per question

The measurement is the one a competition makes: a new process for every instance, the wall-clock time of the
whole answer, three repetitions, the median reported (a scheduling hiccup then decides nothing), cut at the
same ten-second budget as the warm table. This is where start-up appears, and it is what a tool pays when
it shells out to a solver. VIK's JVM column has a twin, the same JVM started from a JDK 25 AOT cache
(JEP 514: one training run over these instances, `bench/aot.sh`), and its native column a twin too, a
GraalVM native image of the same code (`bench/graal.sh`). The cold run builds every one of VIK's binaries
first, from the tree it measures.

**Times in seconds — lower is better;** the best correct answer of a row is green; a run past the budget
reads 🐢, and a solver that rejects the file (a sort or a dialect it does not speak) gets a `—`, as does
SMTInterpol on a script with a `check-sat-assuming`, which its command line can answer wrongly
([external-issues.md](https://github.com/mbovel/vik/blob/main/external-issues.md)).

The floors say most of it: about 220 ms for a JVM process (the JVM's own start-up), about 70 ms for Node with
the WebAssembly module, and about 10 ms for the native binary — the same order as z3 and cvc5 (10–20 ms on
the one-check instances), which are C++ binaries. On the instances that need real work the ranking changes:
the JVM's just-in-time compiler pays off (`NEQ004`: 0.73 s against 1.69 s native), while the native binary
and Node never warm up.

`pspace/ndist.a.20000` is the instance where the fragment matters more than the engine: 20 000-bit vectors,
which VIK lowers to integers and answers in 20 ms as a native binary, while z3 bit-blasts and does not
finish within the budget.

The incremental rows and the `Certora` one draw the solver's current boundary. On `CLEARSY` — 181 checks,
each easy — every solver is fast and VIK's script time is start-up plus 181 small solves; `two_counters`,
`diskperf`, the bit-vector script and `stack_k20` read the same way. On `kind/MESI_i4`, `car_6` and the
`safari` scripts the checks are work: 3.9 s for `car_6`'s 21 checks on the JVM — 5.4 s before the solver
persisted — the native binary over the budget and Node just inside it at 6.8 s, where z3 replays them in
0.10 s and cvc5 in 0.88 s. And the `Certora` instance — `QF_UFDTLIA`, its status unknown to the release —
takes VIK past the budget on every runtime where every other solver answers within about 2 s: datatypes,
functions and integers together are the combination the solver still pays most for (the solvers that
finish agree on `sat`).

## Limitations

- One machine, shared with other users; the numbers are medians and confidence intervals, not guarantees.
- Sixty-one instances, chosen small and inside VIK's fragment. On the competition's hard instances VIK is
  far behind z3 and cvc5, and on some of them it does not finish at all — the
  [corpus](https://github.com/mbovel/vik/tree/main/corpus) records that: of its 6284 files, 396 are marked as
  out of the solver's reach or over the tests' five-second budget.
- The in-process z3 and cvc5 go through the JavaSMT bindings, which cross the JNI boundary per call; their
  binaries are what the cold table measures. Both are reported. cvc5's binding is a snapshot of its main
  rather than a release, for the registry above; z3's frees a solver only when the GC collects its wrapper,
  so the harness resets each solver it is done with — what a program that keeps a context would have to do.
- VIK's answers are checked against the status of every file, on every run of the test suite; a wrong answer
  would show as such in these tables (none does).

## Reproducing

```sh
bench/fetch-native.sh        # once: the z3 and cvc5 libraries and binaries of the versions above (Linux x86-64)
bench/evaluate.sh all        # every solver: the cold run, the warm JMH run, and the tables of this page
bench/evaluate.sh vik        # VIK alone, after a change to it; the cold run builds VIK's binaries first, every platform
bench/evaluate.sh others     # the other solvers alone, after a change of version, replay or instances
bench/evaluate.sh all warm   # one table only: `warm` or `cold` after any of the three
```

A run leaves a CSV file per table in `bench/results/evaluation`, a row per solver and instance, and the
tables are rebuilt from these files.

`bench/evaluation-instances.txt` lists the instances, in the order of both tables; the warm suites, one per
solver in `bench/src/main/scala/io/bovel/bench/evaluation`, list the instances each solver takes and,
commented, the ones it does not and why (the blanked cells); `bench/timeouts.txt` is the ledger of the warm
combinations past the ten-second budget, never run again — so a change that brings one under the budget
removes its line, or its cell stays 🐢.

The raw data of this page is committed in
[`bench/results/evaluation`](https://github.com/mbovel/vik/tree/main/bench/results/evaluation), the samples
of every run in a file of its own: the caption of each table links the files its columns come from.
